Background

Understanding the Mechanics

Essential concepts that make this fraud possible — explained for judges, law enforcement, and the public.

What is CEDE & CO and the Depository Trust Company (DTC)?

CEDE & CO. is the nominee name used by Cede & Company, which is a subsidiary of the Depository Trust Company (DTC). DTC is the largest securities depository in the world, holding over $50 trillion in securities for banks, brokers, and investors.

When you buy or sell stocks through a broker, you never actually receive physical certificates. Instead, DTC holds the "master" certificates, and your broker maintains records showing your beneficial ownership. This is why the documents reference "deposits" into DTC — these are shares being converted from physical certificates into electronic book-entry form that can be traded on public markets.

The significance here: When Cane deposited 36 sequential certificates (Nos. 2029–5323) totaling 2,249,825 shares into DTC, she was making private, hidden stock publicly tradeable. The sequential certificate numbers prove these came from a single controlled source — the Cane family.

What is a CUSIP Number?

A CUSIP (Committee on Uniform Securities Identification Procedures) is a unique 9-character identifier assigned to every publicly traded security in North America. Think of it as a "fingerprint" for stocks and bonds.

Why this matters: A CUSIP number makes a private company's stock an official asset class. Once a company has a CUSIP, institutional investors, pension funds, and foreign entities can theoretically hold or trade it. This is exactly what ICOs did with cryptocurrencies — they created digital tokens that could be traded on exchanges.

By transforming worthless private stock (Tele-Lawyer) through a series of public shells, Cane gave her hidden stock position a CUSIP number — making it a tradeable commodity with real market value, despite having no legitimate business behind it.

What is Registration S?

Regulation S is an SEC rule that allows companies to sell securities to foreign investors without registering with the SEC. The key provision: shares sold under Reg S can be "resold" into the U.S. market after a holding period.

The abuse: This is the critical link that Cane exploited. By registering shares under Reg S, she could legally transfer stock to offshore nominees in Bermuda (LOM Securities), who would then hold until the restriction period expired. At that point, the shares would become freely tradeable in the U.S. market — but the original beneficial owner (Cane) would receive the proceeds offshore, tax-free.

This is the "registration" the original Dynamic Associates implemented immediately — it was the first step in building a mechanism to launder stock into offshore cash.

The Shell Selling Business Model

Why would anyone pay $250,000–$500,000 for a corporate shell? Because it allows them to do something they cannot do otherwise: sell a hidden position.

If you hold 5 million shares of a private company worth nothing, you cannot sell them — there is no market. But if you buy a public shell that's already listed (or get one registered), you can "swap" your private shares into the public entity, then sell them into the market.

The shell seller (like Cane) benefits twice: they sell the shell for $250K–$500K cash, AND they retain their hidden stock position in the resulting public entity, which they later liquidates through offshore accounts.

Buyers benefit: they get instant public company status (saving years of IPO/listing processes), and they can now sell their private holdings to the public market. It's essentially a stock laundering service — turning private, illiquid, worthless stock into public, liquid, tradeable shares.

Like an ICO: Just as ICOs turned digital tokens (often with no underlying value) into tradeable crypto assets, shell company fraud turns worthless private stock into tradeable public shares.

Why Tax-Free?

The Offshore Money Machine

How the entire operation avoided any tax liability.

How Cane Paid Zero Taxes on $6.4 Million

1. Offshore Accounts — Proceeds flowed directly to Bank of Bermuda and NT Butterfield in the Cayman Islands. The U.S. IRS has no jurisdiction and no visibility into these accounts.

2. No FBAR Filings — U.S. persons must file a Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR) for any account exceeding $10,000. Cane and Wallace filed zero FBARs for 21 years — from 2005 through present. Estimated civil penalties alone exceed $72 million.

3. No Capital Gains Reporting — Because the accounts were offshore and the nominees were in Bermuda, there was no U.S. broker reporting to the IRS. The transaction simply never appeared on any U.S. tax return.

4. Registration S Exemption — The original Reg S filing created a legal fiction that the shares were being sold to "foreign investors." Even if the IRS had discovered the accounts, the argument could be made that the gains belonged to foreign entities, not U.S. persons.

The result: $6.4 million in securities fraud proceeds, zero taxes paid, zero disclosure to any U.S. authority.
